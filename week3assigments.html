Extract 01: Questions 31–36 (Dr. Elizabeth Shaw on Geriatric Palliative Care)
Dr. Elizabeth Shaw: My name is Dr. Elizabeth Shaw, and I specialize in geriatric palliative care. Today, I want to address an important but often overlooked aspect of end-of-life care for patients with advanced dementia: recognizing and preserving personhood. It's a challenging area of healthcare, and it's vital that we not only treat the illness, but also respect the individual behind the diagnosis. Personhood, in the context of dementia care, refers to recognizing that even as cognitive abilities decline, the individual's identity, preferences, and life history remain important. Dementia patients, particularly those in the advanced stages, often lose the ability to communicate, make decisions, or express their needs. This can lead to a risk of their identity being overshadowed by their illness. The key to effective palliative care in dementia is maintaining dignity and autonomy despite severe cognitive impairment. This is where the concept of person-centered care becomes essential. As healthcare professionals, we must constantly ask ourselves: how can we ensure that a patient's sense of self and dignity is upheld? How can we honor their wishes, especially when they are no longer able to communicate them clearly? This is particularly critical in dementia care, where the patient's ability to express their preferences is compromised.
In collaboration with Dementia Care Australia, we recently conducted a study to investigate how personhood is managed in end-of-life dementia care. This study involved over 1,200 responses from healthcare professionals, family carers, and patients themselves where possible. The findings highlighted several important gaps in current care approaches. One key issue is the difficulty of making decisions that align with the patient's wishes when those preferences haven't been discussed or documented early enough. Often, families are left guessing what their loved ones might have wanted in terms of treatment decisions, such as the use of antibiotics, pain relief, or resuscitation. This can cause distress for both the family and the patient. The concept of advanced care planning is crucial here. It allows patients to document their preferences while they still have the capacity to do so, ensuring that their values and wishes are respected even in later stages when they can no longer communicate. But unfortunately, as our study revealed, nearly 40% of healthcare professionals have never initiated a conversation about advanced care planning with their dementia patients. This is a significant gap, given the importance of these conversations in upholding patient autonomy and dignity.
Another aspect we must address is the family's involvement in care. The family plays a critical role in dementia care, but they often feel unsupported or unsure of their loved one's wishes. In our survey, 70% of family carers reported feeling uncertain or anxious about the decisions they had to make. Many expressed regret over not having had conversations about end-of-life preferences earlier in the disease's progression. Despite these challenges, there are promising examples of excellent person-centered care. In some cases, healthcare teams have worked closely with families, using the patient's life history, values, and past preferences to guide decisions. This helps ensure that the care remains focused on the individual, not just the disease. However, such examples are still the exception rather than the norm.
Additionally, pain management is another area where upholding personhood becomes essential. Pain is often under-recognized and under-treated in dementia patients, as they may be unable to communicate their discomfort. Our research found that 25% of healthcare professionals were unsure of how to assess pain in non-verbal patients, leading to concerns about under-treatment. Providing adequate pain relief is not just a medical necessity, but a fundamental human right. When it is overlooked, the personhood of the patient is disregarded. Our study also revealed a concerning gap in healthcare professionals' knowledge about patient rights. For example, 20% of respondents were unaware that patients have the right to refuse treatments such as hydration and nutrition at the end of life. Without this knowledge, professionals may fail to respect patient autonomy, further undermining their personhood.
Ultimately, what we need is a comprehensive, well-funded approach to palliative dementia care that prioritizes personhood. This means investing in training for healthcare professionals so they are equipped to discuss and honor patients' wishes. It means supporting families to help them make informed decisions. Most importantly, it means ensuring that the care delivered is not just about managing symptoms, but about preserving the dignity and identity of every person affected by dementia. We still have a long way to go, but with initiatives like advanced care planning and better pain management protocols, we can move towards a model of dementia care that respects and maintains the personhood of patients, even in the most challenging stages of the disease.
Extract 02: Questions 37–42 (Dr. Carter on Celiac Disease Management)
Journalist: I have with me Dr. Carter, an expert in celiac disease, an autoimmune disorder affecting the small intestine. Hello, Dr. Carter, thank you for joining us today.
Dr. Carter: Thank you for inviting me. I'm happy to share insights into my journey and the ongoing challenges surrounding celiac disease, particularly the frequent misdiagnosis it faces.
Journalist: I understand you didn't always focus on celiac disease. Can you share how you transitioned from general internal medicine to specializing in this area?
Dr. Carter: Absolutely. My journey started in internal medicine, focusing primarily on gastrointestinal disorders. However, my career took a turn when my daughter Emily was diagnosed with celiac disease after years of misdiagnoses. For years, she was told she had irritable bowel syndrome, and even a psychological disorder due to the symptoms not fitting the standard profile for celiac disease. Her experience opened my eyes to the difficulties patients face in getting an accurate diagnosis, especially for conditions like celiac disease, which can mimic other disorders.
Journalist: It must have been a challenging time for your family. What finally led to the correct diagnosis?
Dr. Carter: It was a long and frustrating process. Emily's symptoms ranged from abdominal pain to severe fatigue and brain fog. These are non-specific symptoms, which is why doctors initially misdiagnosed her. Eventually, after pushing for more tests, a blood test for celiac markers and a biopsy of her small intestine confirmed the diagnosis. This personal experience drove me to specialize in celiac disease to prevent others from going through what Emily did.
Journalist: Misdiagnosis seems to be a recurring theme with autoimmune diseases. Why do you think that is, particularly with celiac disease?
Dr. Carter: You're absolutely right. One of the biggest challenges with celiac disease is that it mimics so many other conditions. Patients can present with symptoms similar to irritable bowel syndrome (IBS), Crohn's disease, lactose intolerance, or even depression. The symptoms can also vary greatly from one patient to another, which complicates diagnosis further. For example, in children, celiac disease may be misdiagnosed as failure to thrive, whereas in adults, it may be mistaken for anemia or chronic fatigue syndrome.
Journalist: Could you provide some insight into the conditions that are most often mistaken for celiac disease?
Dr. Carter: Certainly. Some of the most common conditions misdiagnosed in place of celiac disease include irritable bowel syndrome, inflammatory bowel disease, lactose intolerance, and non-celiac gluten sensitivity. However, it's not just gastrointestinal conditions. Because celiac disease can also cause symptoms like joint pain and migraines, it's often confused with fibromyalgia or chronic migraines. This wide range of overlapping symptoms is what leads to frequent misdiagnosis.
Journalist: That's a broad spectrum of conditions. Given how common misdiagnosis is, what steps do you recommend for patients who suspect they may have celiac disease but have been diagnosed with something else?
Dr. Carter: The first step is to advocate for yourself and ask for specific testing. A blood test looking for certain antibodies, like tissue transglutaminase antibodies (tTG-IgA), is often the first screening tool. If that test comes back positive, the next step is usually an endoscopic biopsy of the small intestine to look for damage to the villi, which is a hallmark of celiac disease. It's also crucial that patients are still consuming gluten when these tests are done, because stopping gluten can lead to false negatives.
Journalist: How has the awareness and understanding of celiac disease evolved in recent years?
Dr. Carter: Awareness has improved dramatically, but there's still a long way to go. 20 years ago, celiac disease was considered rare, but now we know it affects about 1 in 100 people worldwide. One of the biggest changes is that doctors are becoming more aware of the non-classic symptoms, such as neurological issues, infertility, and osteoporosis, which can all be linked to celiac disease. These symptoms were often overlooked in the past.
Journalist: It's clear that proper diagnosis is critical for effective treatment. What is life like for your daughter now that she has a confirmed diagnosis?
Dr. Carter: Emily is doing very well now, thanks to a strict gluten-free diet. That's the only treatment for celiac disease at the moment, but it has made a world of difference in her life. The fatigue, brain fog, and gastrointestinal symptoms have almost completely disappeared. She's now able to live a normal, healthy life.
Journalist: It's wonderful to hear she's doing so well. Before we wrap up, can you tell us about any ongoing research or developments in the treatment of celiac disease?
Dr. Carter: There's exciting research underway, including efforts to develop a vaccine that could allow people with celiac disease to consume gluten without damaging their intestines. Additionally, there are medications in development aimed at reducing the immune response to gluten. However, for now, the gluten-free diet remains the most effective treatment.
Journalist: Thank you, Dr. Carter, for sharing your insights and personal story with us today. It's been incredibly informative.
Dr. Carter: Thank you for the opportunity to raise awareness about this important issue.
